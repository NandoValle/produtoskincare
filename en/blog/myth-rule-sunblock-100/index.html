Myth vs rule: why "sunblock" and "100% protection" do not exist on Brazilian labels

The myth

"This one is sunblock — it blocks everything."

The rule

No cosmetic in Brazil may promise to block 100% of solar radiation. The law forbids it — and so does physics.

Straight answer: No cosmetic may promise to block 100% of solar radiation in Brazil: the law forbids it, and all protection is partial — which is why sunscreen is reapplied.

The myth

“This one is sunblock, it blocks everything.” “SPF 100, I don't need anything else.” Phrases that still show up in conversation — and, in advertising, a warning sign.

The rule

Resolution RDC No. 629/2022 expressly forbids (art. 14) on sunscreen labels and advertising:

  • “100% protection against UV radiation” or any “anti-sun effect”;
  • the possibility of not reapplying under any circumstances;
  • names that imply total protection or blockage — including “sunblock”.

What exists is a scale: the Protection Category Denomination (DCP). And two technical requirements almost nobody reads: UVA-PF must be at least one third of the SPF, and the critical wavelength at least 370 nm — protecting only against burning (UVB) is not enough; UVA, the aging one, counts too.

The rule

ANVISA Resolution RDC No. 629/2022, art. 14 — forbidden on sunscreen labels and advertising: “100% protection against UV radiation”, any suggestion that reapplication is unnecessary, and names that imply total blockage — including “sunblock”.

What exists is the DCP scale

Low (SPF 6–14.9) · Medium (15–29.9) · High (30–50) · Very high (above 50 and below 100) — with UVA-PF ≥ ⅓ of SPF and a critical wavelength ≥ 370 nm.

How to actually read the packaging

  • Look for the integer SPF and the protection category — both are mandatory.
  • SPF 30 to 50 is the range dermatologists recommend daily; above 50 the gain is small and the temptation to skip reapplication is big.
  • If a packaging promises miracles, be suspicious: Brazilian law requires proof for every claim — and forbids these specifically.

Frequently asked questions

Does SPF 100 protect twice as much as SPF 50?

No. The scale is not linear: above 50 the extra protection is small. What changes the real result is the amount applied and reapplication.

Is “broad spectrum” the same as “sunblock”?

No. Broad spectrum means UVA and UVB coverage. Nothing blocks 100% of solar radiation — and nothing may promise to.

What about tinted sunscreens, for melasma?

The same rules apply. Tinted sunscreens help block visible light, which matters in melasma — and still: reapplication and amount count.
