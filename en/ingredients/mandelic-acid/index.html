Mandelic acid: what it is and how to use it

The gentlest AHA on the shelf — good for starting chemical exfoliation without tearing your skin off. Grounded truth: the studies are small, and sunscreen remains the step that changes everything.

Straight answer

  • A large-molecule AHA: it penetrates more slowly than glycolic acid.
  • Small studies suggest improvement in texture and light spots.
  • Next-day sunscreen is not optional.

Emerging evidence

Clinical trials are small and mostly on peels; for daily cream use, direct evidence is even thinner.

What it is

Mandelic acid is an alpha hydroxy acid (AHA) derived from bitter almond hydrolysate. Its molecule is larger than glycolic acid's — so it absorbs more slowly and is often billed as the “gentler” AHA. It appears in in-office peels and in daily creams and serums.

Exfoliating is not the same as aggressing: mandelic acid works on the skin's surface layers, helping shed dead cells and even out texture.

What studies suggest

Clinical studies on mandelic acid are small and mostly on peels — some with favorable results for melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, including short comparisons with other AHAs. For daily cream use, direct evidence is thinner still: much of what is known comes from the AHA class, not this active alone.

Hence the emerging evidence badge: promising signals and real dermatology practice, but no large independent trials backing strong claims. When choosing between acids, talking to a dermatologist still beats reading the packaging.

Who it suits most

  • Anyone starting chemical exfoliation who wants more tolerance than glycolic usually offers.
  • Uneven texture, dullness or light spots, with a gradual routine.
  • Not the first choice for very reactive skin, active rosacea or open lesions — professional guidance comes first there.

How to use

  • At night, 1–2 times a week at first, increasing as the skin accepts it.
  • Apply after cleansing, on dry skin, and seal with moisturizer.
  • Sunscreen is mandatory the next day: AHAs increase sun sensitivity. Without photoprotection, exfoliating can worsen spots instead of improving them.
  • Introduce one new active at a time and watch the skin for a few weeks.

Cautions and combinations

Pairs well with niacinamida and ceramidas (barrier support). Avoid using it in the same step as salicylic acid, retinol or another exfoliant — alternate nights instead.

Two products with the same ingredient name can be completely different formulations; concentration, pH and stability matter more than the word on the label. If the skin burns, turns red or peels beyond expectation, pause — tingling is not a sign of efficacy.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

There are no robust safety studies for mandelic acid in pregnancy. Common dermatologist practice is to avoid chemical exfoliation in this period and stick to the basics (gentle cleansing, hydration, sunscreen). When in doubt, ask your doctor first.
